## 1. Expressive suppression (masking) versus reappraisal

### What the terms mean

- **Expressive suppression**: feeling the emotion but holding back its outward signs (face, voice,
  words). This is what Penny does.
- **Reappraisal**: reinterpreting the situation so the emotion is smaller to begin with.
- The standard self-report measure is the **Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ)**. It has
  separate suppression and reappraisal scales.

### Core findings

**Gross & John (2003)**, *JPSP* 85, 348–362. Five studies; introduced the ERQ.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12916575/
- Suppressors **express less positive emotion but feel more negative emotion**. Hiding a feeling
  does not make it go away.
- Suppression goes with worse interpersonal functioning (Study 4) and lower well-being (Study 5).
- Reappraisal goes the other way on all of these.
- Recalled but not confirmed from the abstract **(verify)**:
  - 3-month test–retest about .69 for both scales.
  - Men score higher on suppression than women; reappraisal shows no sex difference.
  - Suppressors feel inauthentic.
  - Peers like suppressors less and feel less close to them.
  - Suppression correlates around r ≈ .3 with depressive symptoms.
- Later ERQ studies repeatedly find the male > female difference in suppression; see
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01011/full.
  I found no meta-analytic estimate of its size.

**Gross & Levenson (1997)**, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*. 180 women watched sad, neutral and
amusing films; 90 suppressed their expressions and 90 just watched.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9103721/
- Suppression reduced expressive behaviour in all three films.
- It **raised sympathetic activation of the cardiovascular system** during the emotional films,
  not during the neutral one.
- It reduced amusement but did **not** reduce sadness.

**Webb, Miles & Sheeran (2012)**, "Dealing with feeling", *Psychological Bulletin* 138, 775–808.
A meta-analysis of about 190 studies.
URL: https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432
- Suppressing the **expression** of an emotion worked, with d+ = 0.32 averaged across outcomes.
- Suppressing the **experience** of an emotion did not work (d+ = −0.04), and neither did
  suppressing thoughts about the event (d+ = −0.12).
- Secondary sources summarise the pattern as: suppression lowers expression, barely changes
  experience, and leaves physiology unchanged or raised.
- I could not confirm the separate d values for experience, behaviour and physiology **(verify)**.

**Richards & Gross (2000)**, *JPSP*.
URL: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2000/09/000913203335.htm (press summary)
- Suppression impairs memory for what happened while suppressing. Masking uses up attention.

### Social and relationship costs

**Butler, Egloff, Wilhelm, Smith, Erickson & Gross (2003)**, "The social consequences of
expressive suppression", *Emotion* 3, 48–67. Unacquainted pairs of women discussed an upsetting
film; one of each pair was told to suppress, reappraise, or act naturally.
URL: https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-social-consequences-of-expressive-suppression/
- Suppression disrupted communication.
- It **raised blood pressure in the partner** as well as in the suppressor.
- It **reduced rapport and held back relationship formation**.

**Srivastava, Tamir, McGonigal, John & Gross (2009)**, *JPSP* 96, 883–897. 278 students followed
through their first college term; acquaintances also rated them.
URL: https://pages.uoregon.edu/digital/uonews-archive/node/3017.html (press summary; the study
record is at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4141473/)
- The stable, trait part of suppression predicted lower social support, less closeness and lower
  social satisfaction.
- So did the situational part (suppressing more in the new setting).

**Chervonsky & Hunt (2017)**, "Suppression and expression of emotion in social and interpersonal
outcomes: A meta-analysis", *Emotion* 17, 669–683.
- Suppression had wide-ranging adverse social effects.
- I could not retrieve its pooled r **(verify)**.

**Impett et al. (2012)**, "Suppression sours sacrifice", *PSPB* 38, 707–720. Couples in the lab
plus a 14-day diary.
URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167212437249
- On days when someone suppressed while making a sacrifice for the partner, **both partners**
  reported lower well-being and lower relationship quality.

**The important exceptions**
- **Le & Impett (2013)**: for highly interdependent people, suppressing negative feelings during a
  sacrifice **felt authentic and was beneficial**.
  URL: https://labsites.rochester.edu/lelab/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Le-Impett-2013-Suppressing-negative-emotions-during-sacrifice-feels-authentic-and-is-beneficial-for-the-highly-interdependent.pdf
- **Butler, Lee & Gross (2007)**, *Emotion*: the social costs of suppression were **smaller** for
  women holding more Asian or bicultural values. Partners of European-values suppressors saw them
  as more hostile and withdrawn.
  URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17352561/

So the cost depends on why the person masks. Masking to protect oneself costs the most. Masking to
care for someone the person values costs less, and sometimes nothing.

### Wellbeing and psychopathology

**Aldao, Nolen-Hoeksema & Schweizer (2010)**, *Clinical Psychology Review* 30, 217–237. 114
studies, 241 effect sizes.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20015584/
- Suppression is associated with anxiety and depression.
- Avoidance, rumination and suppression go with more symptoms than acceptance, reappraisal and
  problem solving do.
- The per-strategy effect sizes were behind a paywall **(verify)**.

**Hu et al. (2014)**: meta-analysis of ERQ studies.
URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00135/full
(secondary citation)
- Reappraisal correlated r = .26 with positive mental-health indicators and r = −.20 with negative
  ones.
- Suppression ran the opposite way. The secondary source calls the effects "moderate to strong",
  but I did not get suppression's own r **(verify)**.

**Chapman, Fiscella, Kawachi, Duberstein & Muennig (2013)**, *Journal of Psychosomatic Research* 75,
381–385. 729 adults followed for 12 years, 111 deaths.
URL: https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/41288133
- Comparing the 75th with the 25th percentile of emotion suppression:
  - all-cause mortality HR = 1.35 (95% CI 1.00–1.82, p = .049);
  - cancer mortality HR = 1.70.
- One study with borderline significance. Treat it as weak evidence.

### Does masked hurt build up and lead to withdrawal or depression?

This is the most important question for the simulation, and the evidence is **mixed on
direction**.

**Larsen, Vermulst, Geenen et al. (2013)**, *Journal of Early Adolescence*. 1,753 adolescents, mean
age 13.8, measured one year apart.
URL: https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/emotion-regulation-in-adolescence-a-prospective-study-of-expressi/
- **Depressive symptoms predicted more suppression a year later.**
- **Suppression did not predict later depression.**

A three-wave study of 2,051 adolescents ("Social coping by masking?",
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3492695/):
- Depression lowered perceived parental support.
- Lower support then raised suppression.
- This chain held for girls, not for boys.

The causal story with better support is: **feeling low and unsupported leads to masking, and
masking makes relationships worse** (Srivastava 2009; Butler 2003). Those worse relationships
plausibly feed the low mood. "Bottled-up hurt accumulates until it causes depression" is a
popular idea; direct prospective support for it is **weak**.

**Thought-suppression rebound**: Abramowitz, Tolin & Street (2001), *Clinical Psychology Review* 21,
683–703; 28 studies.
URL: https://contextualscience.org/publications/paradoxical_effects_thought_suppression_metaanalysis_controlled_studies
- A **small-to-moderate rebound**: suppressed thoughts come back more after suppression ends.
- This is about thoughts, not facial expression. I found no meta-analysis showing a rebound of
  masked emotional expression.

**Emotional inertia**: Kuppens, Allen & Sheeber (2010), *Psychological Science*; Brose et al. (2015),
*Cognition and Emotion*, 100 days of ratings.
URL: https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8q187/emotional-inertia-contributes-to-depressive-symptoms-beyond-perseverative-thinking
- Negative affect that is slow to change from one day to the next goes with depression and low
  self-esteem, beyond rumination. This fits a "held" store that decays slowly.

**Maltreatment raises suppression.** A large meta-analysis summarised in a 2022 Frontiers article
(https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.994065/text):
- Childhood maltreatment goes with less reappraisal and more rumination and suppression.
- These strategies partly mediate the link from maltreatment to symptoms.
- Several single studies find that suppression specifically mediates emotional abuse → depression.
- I did not obtain the authors or pooled numbers **(verify)**.

### How stable is suppression? Is it heritable?

- **Stability**: Srivastava et al. (2009) separated a stable component that held across a major life
  transition from a situational component. Gross & John's retest of about .69 over 3 months is
  unconfirmed **(verify)**. Suppression behaves like a trait with a real situational part.
- **Heritability**: McRae, Rhee, Gatt, Godinez, Williams & Gross (2017), twin study, 743 pairs.
  URL: https://vivo-cub.colorado.edu/display/pubid_157422
  - Suppression: a² = .35, e² = .65.
  - Reappraisal: a² = .20, e² = .80.
  - Neuroticism (for comparison): a² = .41.
  - Shared family environment contributed essentially nothing.
  - So roughly a third of the variation in masking is genetic, and the rest comes from experiences
    siblings do not share.

## 2. Internalizing versus externalizing

**Achenbach, Ivanova, Rescorla, Turner & Althoff (2016)**, *JAACAP* 55, 647–656. A review of more
than 75,000 articles using the internalizing/externalizing split, ages 1½–18.
URL: https://www.brynmawr.edu/news/journal-article-professor-psychology-leslie-rescorla-1
- **Internalizing** means anxious, depressed, withdrawn and somatic problems.
- **Externalizing** means aggressive and rule-breaking behaviour.
- The two scales correlate positively; many children show both.

**Caspi et al. (2014)**, "The p factor", *Clinical Psychological Science* 2, 119–137 (Dunedin cohort,
adolescence to midlife).
URL: https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-p-factor-one-general-psychopathology-factor-in-the-structure-/
- Disorders fit three factors: internalizing, externalizing and thought disorder.
- They fit one general factor (p) even better.
- For a simulation: inward and outward are **not opposite ends of one scale**. One overall level of
  distress, plus a direction, is closer to the data.

### Predictors

**Temperament**
- High negative affectivity predicts both internalizing and externalizing problems.
- Low effortful control predicts externalizing.
- High effortful control weakens the step from fear to internalizing and from frustration to
  externalizing.
- Source: Rothbart-tradition studies, e.g.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732742. I found no single meta-analysis with pooled
  numbers.

**Behavioural inhibition (shyness)**: Sandstrom, Uher & Pavlova (2020), *Journal of Abnormal Child
Psychology* 48, 57–66; 27 studies.
URL: https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/handle/10222/79753
- Behaviourally inhibited children have higher odds of any later anxiety disorder: OR = 2.80.
- For social anxiety the odds ratio is 5.84.
- Clauss & Blackford (2012), as cited there: only about 40% of inhibited children develop social
  anxiety disorder.
- **Shy children lean inward.**

**Sex**
- Boys score higher on externalizing at about ages 6–10.
- Girls score higher on internalizing from about age 10.4 onward.
- The interaction effects were modest (ΔR² ≈ .03–.05). CBCL analysis:
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12581308/

**Parenting**: Pinquart (2017), *Developmental Psychology* 53, 873–932; meta-analysis.
- Harsh control, psychological control, and authoritarian or neglectful styles go with more
  externalizing.
- Authoritative parenting goes with less externalizing.
- I could not retrieve the specific r values **(verify)**.

**Coercion**: Patterson's coercive family process.
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4362845
- Parent and child each reinforce the other's aversive behaviour, and escalation is the norm until
  one side "wins".
- This is the best-supported account of how a harsh parent and a **fighting-back** child lock in.

### Stability and heritability

- In an adoption sample followed from early adolescence into adulthood, internalizing correlated
  r = .34–.58 over time and externalizing r = .47–.69.
  URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3E70EED8ADFFA8E51940DDE0BFAB5031/S1832427400007556a.pdf/longitudinal-genetic-analysis-of-internalizing-and-externalizing-problem-behavior-in-adopted-biologically-related-and-unrelated-sibling-pairs.pdf
- Externalizing is the more stable of the two.
- Early-onset externalizing: a meta-analysis found stability of .12–.52, mostly around .30, and
  more lasting in boys. URL: https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/handle/1803/14422
- Heritability is about one third for each when twin and family designs are combined (Nikstat &
  Riemann 2020, *PLOS ONE*; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7089526/). Twin-only and
  same-rater designs give higher figures.

## 3. Growing up with a harsh, neglectful or alcoholic parent

### How often harsh parenting passes to the next generation

**Kaufman & Zigler (1987)**, *American Journal of Orthopsychiatry*.
- About **30% (±5%)** of people maltreated as children go on to maltreat their own children.
- Cited at https://aifs.gov.au/resources/policy-and-practice-papers/intergenerational-transmission-maltreatment

**Assink et al. (2018)**, *Clinical Psychology Review*; three-level meta-analysis of 84 studies and
285 effect sizes.
URL: https://dare.uva.nl/id/40cbb197-06ca-4783-a5c3-453b054cec1b
- Pooled r = .29.
- The odds of maltreating are 2.99 times higher if the parent was maltreated.
- Better-quality studies give smaller effects, and there are signs of publication bias.

**Madigan et al. (2019)**, *Development and Psychopathology*; 142 studies, 227,918 dyads.
URL: https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10070319
- Transmission is specific to the type of maltreatment:
  - neglect d = 0.24;
  - physical abuse d = 0.41;
  - **emotional abuse d = 0.57** (the largest);
  - sexual abuse d = 0.39.
- The authors describe these effects as modest.

**Madigan, Turgeon, Racine, Zhu, Alink, Ereyi-Osas & Fearon (2025)**, *Development and
Psychopathology* 37, 2590–2611.
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/development-and-psychopathology/article/abs/patterns-of-continuity-and-discontinuity-of-childhood-maltreatment-across-generations-a-metaanalysis/F4E2965748FFDC7334028D48436F584F
- Proportions across families:

  | Group | Share |
  |---|---|
  | Cycle maintainers (maltreated, and maltreat) | 17.1% |
  | **Cycle breakers** (maltreated, do not maltreat) | **23.6%** |
  | Cycle initiators (not maltreated, but maltreat) | 11.4% |
  | Neither | 47.8% |

- Of maltreated parents, about 17.1 / (17.1 + 23.6) ≈ **42% maintain the cycle and 58% break it**.
  That is my arithmetic from the pooled proportions; it is not a figure from the paper.

**Widom, Czaja & DuMont (2015)**, *Science*; 30-year prospective study.
URL: https://calio.dspacedirect.org/handle/11212/3797?show=full
- Maltreated people were more often reported to child protective services.
- They did not **self-report** more physical or sexual abuse of their children.
- Part of the measured "transmission" is detection bias.

**Why most do not repeat it**
- Egeland, Jacobvitz & Sroufe (1988), *Child Development*, "Breaking the cycle of abuse": mothers
  who broke the cycle more often had an emotionally supportive adult in childhood, therapy, or a
  supportive partner. I did not confirm the exact rates **(verify)**.
- Werner's Kauai study: of 49 children of alcoholics raised in chronic poverty, **59% coped well
  by age 18**. Resilient children had communication skills, a drive to achieve, and the ability to
  draw positive attention from other adults.
  URL: https://www.mdedge.com/psychiatry/article/23617/pediatrics/building-resilience-children-alcoholics/page/0/1

**Children who go the opposite way.** Staub & Vollhardt (2008) and Vollhardt (2009) review
"**altruism born of suffering**": victimisation can increase helping, especially toward people who
are suffering in similar ways. It is more likely when the person was helped, had caring others, or
had prosocial models.
URL: https://commons.clarku.edu/faculty_psychology/675
- This is a theory-and-review literature with no pooled effect size. Treat it as moderate,
  conditional evidence.

### Parentification and compulsive caregiving

**Jurkovic (1997)**, *Lost Childhoods* (book).
- Distinguishes **instrumental** parentification (chores, caring for siblings) from **emotional**
  parentification (being the parent's confidant or the regulator of the parent's mood).
- Distinguishes **adaptive** parentification (limited, acknowledged, not overtaxing) from
  **destructive** parentification.
- This is clinical theory, not a meta-analysis.

**Hooper, DeCoster, White & Voltz (2011)**, *Journal of Clinical Psychology* 67, 1028–1043; only 12
studies.
URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40653-021-00411-8 (secondary citation)
- Childhood parentification correlated positively with adult depression, anxiety, substance use,
  PTSD, personality and eating problems.
- The correlation was **small**.

**Bowlby on compulsive caregiving.** Bowlby described caring for a parent in childhood as the
antecedent of an adult pattern of **compulsive caregiving**: giving care, being unable to ask for
it. West & Sheldon-Keller operationalised four patterns (compulsive self-reliance, compulsive
caregiving, compulsive care-seeking, angry withdrawal).
URL: https://psychotherapy.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.psychotherapy.1991.45.3.425
(case study)
- I found no meta-analysis tying compulsive caregiving to parentification.
- Evidence: **theoretical plus small studies**.

### Children of alcoholics, and the "hero / caretaker" roles

**Outcomes are well documented.**
- Parental alcoholism raises externalizing problems in children (large, consistent distal effects
  in longitudinal work) and also internalizing problems.
  URL: https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/3ba7b0f9-86e4-47b3-9983-c3d05b08d593
- Risk is highest with two alcoholic parents or an antisocial alcoholic parent.

**The ACoA role literature is largely pop psychology.**
- Wegscheider (1981) and Black (1982) proposed Hero, Scapegoat, Lost Child and Mascot. Woititz
  (1983) listed 13 ACoA traits. These came from clinical observation and self-help writing.
- Logue, Sher & Frensch (1992), "Purported characteristics of adult children of alcoholics: A
  possible 'Barnum effect'", *Professional Psychology: Research and Practice*:
  - Students rated ACoA descriptions as highly self-descriptive whether or not their parents were
    alcoholic.
  - The authors judged the traits too vague to separate groups.
  - The citation details are from memory and the summary is from secondary sources
    **(verify page numbers)**.
  - URL of a secondary description: https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bfcc1596-a950-45a7-adb3-dab9f90ed85a/content
- Studies of the roles themselves (e.g. https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/23635;
  https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/items/0ef18c17-e796-4242-878b-7636c5f16e70):
  - People who grew up with parental alcoholism did not differ from controls in which role they
    took or how strongly.
  - People from **dysfunctional or abusive families** scored higher on Scapegoat and Lost Child.
    They were about three times as likely to have Lost Child as their main role.
  - So the roles track **family dysfunction and abuse, not alcoholism as such**. The "hero /
    caretaker" type in particular has little specific support.

For Penny, the evidence-based ingredients are:
- harsh and emotionally abusive parenting;
- parentification;
- a shy, inhibited temperament.

The ACoA "hero" label adds nothing that is supported.

## 4. Conflict in harsh households: who answers back, who absorbs

**How often conflict happens.** Laursen, Coy & Collins (1998), *Child Development* 69, 817–832;
meta-analysis of normal families.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9680687
- The **rate** of parent–child conflict falls from early to late adolescence.
- Its **emotional intensity** rises from early to mid adolescence.
- I found no figure for how much more often conflict happens in harsh or alcoholic households
  specifically (no source found).

**Who answers back.**
- Coercion theory (Patterson): a child with high frustration and low effortful control answers
  back. Parent and child escalate, and this predicts antisocial outcomes.
- An inhibited or shy child is more likely to lean inward, by temperament, behavioural-inhibition
  data and sex differences (section 2).

**Children's responses to conflict between parents.** Davies & Cummings' **emotional security
theory**.
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3918896
- Children react to destructive conflict with **fear, avoidance, and involvement** (stepping in,
  mediating).
- Insecurity mediates the link from conflict to child problems in longitudinal studies, ages 5–18.
- Involvement is the research cousin of "absorbing" and "appeasing".

**Is "fawning" real?**
- The term comes from therapist Pete Walker (2003 article; 2013 book *Complex PTSD*). Walker was
  not a researcher.
- The Conversation's expert summary says plainly there is **not much research** on it
  (https://theconversation.com/what-is-fawning-how-is-it-related-to-trauma-and-the-fight-or-flight-response-205024).
- The nearest research concepts:
  - **Submissive and appeasement behaviour.** Gilbert and colleagues: on the Early Life
    Experiences Scale (n = 225), only the "submissiveness" factor of childhood recall predicted
    depression. Submissive behaviour goes with depression proneness and unfavourable parenting.
    URL: https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/cpp.359
  - **Involvement responses** (Davies & Cummings).
  - **Tend-and-befriend** (Taylor 2000) as a stress response.
- Verdict: the behaviour (appeasing a threatening person) is real and measured. The "fourth F"
  framing as a distinct survival reflex is **pop psychology without direct evidence**.

**What absorbing does to the absorber over time.** Pieced together from the sources above:
- The submissiveness factor predicted depression (Gilbert).
- Emotional abuse is the type of maltreatment most strongly tied to later depression (Humphreys et
  al. 2020: maltreatment and depression diagnosis g = 1.07; daslab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca
  PDF linked from search).
- Emotional abuse raises rejection sensitivity (r = .275; see section 5).
- Maltreatment raises habitual suppression, which mediates part of the step to depression.
- Suppression costs closeness outside the home (Srivastava 2009).

**Displacement.** Marcus-Newhall, Pedersen, Carlson & Miller (2000), *JPSP*; 49 studies; d = 0.55.
URL: https://www.academia.edu/71536430
- Provoked people who cannot retaliate against the source aggress more against an innocent party.
- This is a well-supported route by which held anger can come out sideways. For a gentle masker
  the research suggests withdrawal is more typical; displacement is the route for people who are
  bolder but blocked.

## 5. Trust after maltreatment, and "all or nothing" judgement

### Slow trust

- **Adult attachment.** Li, Ma, Cao, Liu, Qi & Chen (2026), *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*; 211
  studies, 82,376 people.
  URL: https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/childhood-maltreatment-and-adult-attachment-a-three-level-meta-analytic-review/
  - Childhood maltreatment correlates with attachment **anxiety r = .23** and **avoidance
    r = .20**.
  - The links are stronger for emotional maltreatment.
- **Rejection sensitivity.** Gao, Assink, Chongzeng & Chan (2024), *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*; 16
  studies.
  URL: https://dare.uva.nl/id/88d96f73-7b41-490b-92aa-6caadc270366
  - Overall r = .23; **emotional abuse r = .275**; physical abuse r = .157.
- **Self-reported versus behavioural trust.** Gobin & Freyd (2014).
  URL: https://dynamic.uoregon.edu/jjf/articles/gobinfreyd2014.pdf
  - High betrayal-trauma exposure lowered self-reported general and partner trust.
  - It did **not** change behaviour in the economic Trust Game.
  - Other studies find maltreated people lower their distrust less after positive feedback, and
    maltreated adolescent girls are worse at **learning** who is trustworthy (summary at
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7856450/).
  - So "slow trust" is better modelled as **slower updating toward trust**, plus a lower stated
    prior, than as refusing to cooperate.
- **Childhood hardship and adult trust.** Mell, Safra, Demange, Algan, Baumard & Chevallier (2020;
  N = 915 and N = 66,281): lower childhood socioeconomic status predicted lower adult social trust.
  URL: https://hal-sciencespo.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03393061
- **"Testing" behaviour.** No quantitative source found. It is a clinical description.

### Trust asymmetry and integrity violations

- **Slovic (1993)**, "Perceived risk, trust, and democracy", *Risk Analysis* 13, 675–682.
  URL: https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/d50d56e1-a110-40c6-81bc-a1178c97bd31
  - The **asymmetry principle**: negative events lower trust far more than positive events raise
    it. "Trust is much easier to destroy than to create."
- **Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004)**, *Journal of Applied Psychology*.
  URL: https://source.washu.edu/2004/09/removing-the-shadow-of-suspicion/
  - After a **competence** violation, an apology repairs trust better.
  - After an **integrity** violation, a denial works better than an apology. An apology confirms
    a character flaw, and people weigh that one negative act above later good behaviour.

### All-or-nothing moral judgement (protected or sacred values)

- **Baron & Spranca (1997)**, "Protected values", *OBHDP* 70, 1–16.
  URL: https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP19970407.html
  - Protected values resist trade-offs.
  - They show **quantity insensitivity**: one violation is about as bad as many.
  - They come with a sense of obligation and concern with one's own participation.
  - That they also come with anger is recalled, not confirmed **(verify)**.
- **Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green & Lerner (2000)**, *JPSP* 78, 853–870.
  URL: https://www.econlib.org/tetlock-the-prophet/ (secondary)
  - Taboo trade-offs triggered **moral outrage**: harsh character judgements, anger and contempt,
    and wanting to punish.
  - They also triggered **moral cleansing**: afterwards participants wanted to reaffirm the value,
    for example by volunteering.
  - Decision makers who even **took longer** to weigh a sacred value were judged more harshly.
  - Outrage softened when a violation was reframed as a tragic or routine trade-off.
  - I could not retrieve scale means **(no magnitudes obtained)**.
- **Ginges, Atran, Medin & Shikaki (2007)**, *PNAS*.
  URL: https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/45668
  - Offering money to compromise a sacred value **backfired**, raising support for violence and
    opposition.
  - Symbolic gestures such as a sincere apology or respect for the value created flexibility.
- **Skitka, Bauman & Sargis (2005)**, *JPSP* 88, 895–917.
  URL: https://razzari.notion.site/Skitka-Bauman-and-Sargis-2005-68973c80d5f5412787df5de8c8abbcf9
  - Strong **moral conviction** predicted:
    - more preferred social and physical distance from people who disagree;
    - intolerance of them as friends and even as shopkeepers;
    - less goodwill;
    - less ability to agree on procedures.
  - This held beyond attitude extremity, importance and centrality.
- **Dichotomous thinking.** A Japanese study of 41,284 residents tied high-crime childhood
  environments to dichotomous thinking. A study of 981 students tied PTSD symptoms to it.
  Correlational; no meta-analysis found.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710117

**Summary for Penny.** "All or nothing when a held value is violated" is well supported as a
general human pattern for **moral convictions**, independent of her history:
- it is insensitive to how big the violation was;
- it produces outrage and distancing;
- money or excuses make it worse.

Whether maltreatment makes people **more** all-or-nothing is weakly supported.

## Mechanisms a simulation could use

Each rule lists its inputs, outputs, rough magnitudes and what backs it. The magnitudes are design
guesses scaled to the effect sizes above, not measured constants.

### M1. Expression trait splits each felt affect

- **Inputs**
  - Felt affect `f`: signed; hurt negative, warmth positive. It comes from the existing appraisal
    using sensitivity and self-regard.
  - Trait `expression` e ∈ [0, 1], fixed per NPC. Penny about 0.25, Pam about 0.85.
  - Context.
- **Effective expression**:

  `e_eff = e × (1 − 0.35·threat) × (1 − 0.25·kin) × (1 − 0.2·lowMood)`, clamped to [0.05, 1].

  | Input | Meaning | Grounding |
  |---|---|---|
  | `threat` (0–1) | the other's power or hostility toward this person | submissive behaviour toward higher-rank others (Gilbert) |
  | `kin` | 1 for one's own family | |
  | `lowMood` (0–1) | how low the person's mood is | depression → more suppression a year later (Larsen 2013) |

- **Outputs**
  - `shown = f × e_eff`
  - `held = f − shown`
- **Asymmetry by valence.** Suppression cuts positive expression too (Gross & John), so the same
  split applies to warmth. A masker's warmth is under-seen, and a masker is under-liked.

### M2. What each part drives

**Shown part**
- It is all that other NPCs perceive.
- It feeds the desire gate (argue or answer back) and others' regard toward this person.
- An observer's regard update uses `shown`, never `f`.

**Mood**
- Mood takes `f × (1 − 0.1·(1 − e_eff))`, not `shown`. Hiding barely lowers felt experience: Webb
  2012, d ≈ 0 for suppressing experience, and Gross & John's suppressors feel more negative
  emotion, not less.

**Held part**, into a per-NPC reservoir `R` (negative affect only):
- `R += |held|` when f < 0.
- Decay: `R *= (1 − λ)` per day, with λ about 0.10 × (1 − retention). High retention means slow
  decay, matching emotional inertia (Kuppens 2010).
- Stance: `stance −= 0.3 × held_norm`, pushing toward withdrawn.
- Silent regard: regard toward the source falls by `0.6 × |held|`. Shown hurt lowers regard by
  `1.0 ×`, because some of it is discharged in the exchange (keep this as a tunable). The other
  person never sees the drop.

### M3. What the held reservoir costs over time

| Cost | Rule | Magnitude | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower mood baseline | `moodBase −= k·R` | k ≈ 0.02–0.05 per unit, capped; a few points of mood over weeks | suppression r ≈ .2–.3 with depressive symptoms (verify); direction contested |
| Mood lowers masking further (loop) | `lowMood` in `e_eff` above | 20% lower e at worst mood | Larsen 2013; parental-support mediation |
| Less closeness | others' warmth gains from interactions with this NPC × (0.75 + 0.25·e) | 25% slower bonding at e = 0 | Srivastava 2009; Butler 2003 |
| Effort cost | power of acting −0.05 × held per encounter, recovered overnight | small | sympathetic activation (Gross & Levenson 1997); memory cost (Richards & Gross 2000) |
| Partner strain | the other party's mood −0.05 when this NPC masks a strong affect in a face-to-face argument | small | Butler 2003 partner blood pressure; Impett 2012 |
| Withdrawal | avoidance and go-home probability + 0.1 × R_norm | moderate | stance and avoidance pathway; emotional security avoidance |
| Mortality or illness | **do not model** | | single study, HR 1.35 |

**Exception: caring masking** (Le & Impett 2013; Butler, Lee & Gross 2007). When the NPC masks
**for** someone they value (high regard plus high understanding) rather than out of threat:
- halve the closeness and mood costs;
- the held part does not lower regard toward that person.

This lets Penny mask with children and friends at low cost but pay full price with Pam.

### M4. Outflow from the reservoir (rare, gated)

The research supports two outflows, not a mandatory "explosion".

1. **Rupture** (direct). If `R > θ_rupture` and boldness is at least moderate, the desire gate can
   fire against the source. Probability:

   `p = 0.05 × (R/θ − 1) × boldness` per encounter, capped at 0.25.

   - `R` then drops by half; regard and stance update from the shown outburst.
   - For a family pair, keep the rule that families do not answer through the gate. Add a separate
     **household argument** event with the same probability. This gives Sid real arguments in
     Penny's home that are rare and costly.
   - Grounding: thought-suppression rebound is small to moderate (Abramowitz 2001). Treat ruptures
     as rare.
2. **Displacement** (sideways). If `R` is high and the source is high-threat (kin or powerful), a
   curt act can land on a low-threat bystander. Probability ~0.5 × rupture probability.
   - Grounding: Marcus-Newhall 2000, d = 0.55, but those studies were on provoked people in
     general.
   - For a low-boldness, high-understanding NPC like Penny, scale it down by understanding:
     `× (1 − understanding)`.

### M5. Internalizer and externalizer direction

- Do not make this a separate axis. It falls out of `expression × boldness` with a shared distress
  level (p factor):
  - **low e, low boldness**: internalizer (withdraw, mask, reservoir builds). Penny.
  - **high e, high boldness**: externalizer (lashes out, little held). Pam.
  - **high e, low boldness**: shows hurt but does not fight (cries, complains).
  - **low e, high boldness**: cold and controlled, with rare sharp ruptures.
- **Coercion loop for externalizer parent vs absorbing child.** When the parent's shown hostility
  is met with appeasement (no shown counter-affect), the parent's urge to repeat is **not**
  reduced, because nothing stops her. When it is met with a shown counter, there is escalation.
  This reproduces Patterson's pattern.

### M6. Caregiving overflow (Penny's kindness to children)

- Input: a history flag `parentified` (or a "raised by a harsh parent" flag) plus high
  understanding.
- Output: a bonus to prosocial acts toward low-power or vulnerable others (children, the new,
  people seen hurting):
  - +0.1 to +0.2 on the act's score;
  - a small mood gain on doing it, so it partly drains R at maybe 0.5 × the act's warmth.
- Evidence: altruism born of suffering (review, conditional); compulsive caregiving (theory, small
  studies). **Weak to moderate**, so keep it small. Hooper (2011) finds the cost side of
  parentification is real but small.

### M7. Slow trust and the all-or-nothing value breach

**Trust update**
- Positive evidence raises regard × `τ`, with τ ≈ 0.75 for maltreated-history NPCs and 1.0
  otherwise. This is scaled to attachment and rejection-sensitivity r ≈ .2–.28.
- Negative evidence lowers regard by 1.5–3× the size of an equal positive event (Slovic asymmetry).
  Use the same multiplier for everyone; history changes the gain on positives, not the asymmetry.

**Protected value**
- Each NPC may hold 0–2 protected values; for Penny, perhaps "honesty" or "kindness to children".
- A witnessed violation of one by someone triggers:
  - **Fixed large drop**: a regard drop that **ignores magnitude**, e.g. a fixed −0.5 on a −1..1
    scale, the same whether the act was small or large (Baron & Spranca quantity insensitivity).
  - **Distance**: a raised avoidance target for that person (Skitka social distance).
  - **Integrity flag**: a "seen as integrity breach" mark on the pair. While it is set:
    - later positive acts from that person count at × 0.3 (Kim et al. 2004);
    - **material** amends such as gifts count at 0 or slightly negative (Ginges 2007 backfire);
    - an acknowledgement or show of respect for the value is the only thing that lowers the flag.
  - **Cleansing**: a small boost to acts that affirm the value, shortly after (Tetlock 2000).
- Penny's version is masked: she shows only a cool, polite greeting. The drop goes through `held`,
  so others do not see it coming. That is the "coolly polite" look.

### M8. The first greeting tone (feature built but switched off)

- A curt greeting produces `f` = small hurt. For Penny (e ≈ 0.25) about 75% is held.
- Her greeting back stays neutral, so the other NPC perceives nothing.
- Her mood dips by the full amount, R grows a little, and regard drops silently.
- This is the case Sid describes. It needs no new act, only the split.

### Calibration note

The literature gives mostly **correlations of .2–.3** and **odds ratios of about 3** for these
links. Large individual differences are the norm, and most people from harsh homes do not repeat
the harshness. Keep every rule's effect modest and probabilistic, so that two NPCs with the same
history can turn out differently. Then let the seeded draw decide which way each NPC goes.
